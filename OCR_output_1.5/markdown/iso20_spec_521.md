#### H.1.5 Cross certification

Cross certification is a technical solution for organizations to extend the trust relationship of their members to another organization. For example, without cross-certification, the organization A's member Alice cannot trust another organization B's member Bob because Bob's certificate is issued by a CA of organization B but Alice can only trust the CA of the organization A. If the CA of A and CA of B cross-certify each other, Alice's trust relationship can be extended such that Alice can now trust any member of organization B, and vice versa. Here Alice trusting Bob means that Alice can validate Bob's certificate such that Alice finds Bob's certificate directly or indirectly signed by a CA that Alice already trusts.

Cross certification can be a valuable mechanism in V2G systems. For a V2G session to be established, EV should be able to validate the SECC's certificate, which is issued under a particular V2G root CA. If the EV does not hold the corresponding V2G root CA certificate, a secure channel cannot be established. However, the EV manufacturer cannot anticipate all the charging operators their clients will visit, and even so, EV may not be able to store all the necessary V2G root CA certificates. In this context, cross-certification between the certificate chains issued through the multiple V2G root CAs can open the door for cross-V2G charging services. For example, if a German V2G root CA and a French V2G root CA cross-certify each other, any EV that holds only German V2G root CA certificate in its storage can charge from an EVSE that has an SECC certificate issued under the French V2G root CA certificate.

Examples provided here (and in various subclauses) are only one way of cross-certification and many variants can be chosen by implementers. For example, cross-certification can happen at sub-CA level to limit the scope of the trust extension. Cross certification is not necessarily mutual; one V2G root CA may cross certify another, but not vice versa. Cross certification is also not limited to V2G certificates. Applying cross certification to other types of certificates, such as OEM-issued certificates, and eMSP-issued certificates is up to the business decisions by relevant organizations. Provided below are examples of the different kinds of cross-certifications. In addition, the advantages and disadvantages of cross-certification are provided.

This document neither mandates cross-certification nor prevents it at any level. Cross certification is possible at root CA or at sub-CA levels. This document simply opens the door for the possibility of cross-certification in case the industry chooses to do so. To that end, this document allows a certificate chain to contain up to 4 certificates (one leaf, two sub-CAs, and one cross-certificate) when a cross certificate is used (refer to requirement IETF RFC 6712) provides details of a protocol that can be used to generate cross-certificates.

##### H.1.5.1 Mutual cross certification

If V2G root CA1 and V2G root CA2 want to cross-certify each other, V2G root CA1 issues a cross-certificate for V2G root CA2's public key according to the certificate profile defined in Annex B, and V2G root CA2 does the same for V2G root CA1. When an EV that trusts only V2G root CA1 visits an EVSE whose certificate is issued by V2G root CA2, EV first indicates that it trusts V2G root CA1 in its TLS ClientHello message (see [V2G20-1006]). Then SECC provides its own certificate chain along with the cross-certificate issued by the V2G root CA1 in its TLS ServerHello message. Now EV can validate the SECC's certificate chain without having to trust the V2G root CA2's certificate. Since two V2G Roots are cross-certifying in both direction, this is an example of a mutual cross-certification.

When business model requires, V2G root CA1 can instead cross-certify a set of sub-CAs under V2G root CA2 and vice versa, limiting the scope of extended trust.
